    Engines¶

    This page helps you choose the numerical engine (the backend) for a study and tells you where each engine must not be used. Start with the decision path; the tables below it give the accuracy and the cost behind each choice, cited from the validation page, the engine pages, the performance page and the known limitations. The second half of the page explains the physics the engines share and where they differ.

    All engines solve one physical problem: the quasi-static current field of electrodes and conductors in horizontally layered soil, below 1 kHz. They share the data model (World), the solve pipeline and the network equations, so the same world can be solved by any of them and the results compared. They differ in the soils they accept, in how they represent the current on the wires, in accuracy and in cost.

    Choosing an engine¶

    Accuracy against references¶

    Every engine is compared with references that do not use a groundfield solver (closed forms, an independent thin-wire solver, high-precision quadrature, the solid electrodes of fem) in the benchmark suite B1–B13 of the validation page. Typical deviations, signed where the sign is known:

    How to read it:

    • image is exact for its own model. For a straight wire the line kernel reproduces the closed-form resistance of a uniform line current to about 1e-6 at any segment length; a ring converges with the square of the segment length (kernel of the image family). What remains is the error of the ansatz: about 1 % on impedances of rods, rings, grids and rectangles, more on stars, and up to tens of per cent on surface potentials next to grid corners and star tips — with the step voltage under-estimated, the non-conservative direction (field quantities).
    • mom resolves the current distribution but not the absolute value. Its point-source near field leaves impedances 0.6 … 1.9 % low and drifts as the segments get shorter. Its strength is the shape of the current and of the surface potential, which is why it is the reference for field quantities and for the current model of the image family.
    • mom_sommerfeld checks the kernel. It reproduces the Tagg/Sunde series of mom to 1.2e-8 on the two-layer worlds of B6 and the three-layer kernel to 2.3e-13 of a 30-digit quadrature (B5); its tables for three layers move a grid's impedance by 1.5e-10 (B12). Its discretisation error is that of mom.
    • fem is the independent reference. The axisymmetric model agrees with Weber's disc to 3.1e-4 and with the thin-wire rod and ring to better than 1e-4 (B1–B3); the 3-D model agrees with the axisymmetric one to about 2e-3 away from layer interfaces and other wires, and reports an error estimate of 3 … 5e-3.

    What the table and the performance page show:

    • Up to a few thousand segments image and mom take a second or less. The line kernel of the image family integrates near pairs exactly, which costs about what the dense LU of mom costs.
    • mom grows faster. Its LU is \(O(N^3)\) and it holds more of the dense system in memory (3.6 GiB against 1.0 GiB at 9 626 segments); with inductive conductors it factorises once per frequency (twice the time of image for 11 frequencies above).
    • image_2layer pays for contrast and thin top layers. The image series needs 20 terms at \(\lvert K\rvert\) = 0.5 and 277 at 0.94; a 40-house network takes 3.2 s in 500 / 100 Ω·m with \(h_1\) = 5 m and 4.0 s in 1000 / 30 Ω·m with \(h_1\) = 1.5 m, where Engine.image_far_field = "point" brings it to 2.0 s for catalogue sweeps.
    • mom_sommerfeld integrates every distinct pair. In two-layer soil it costs several times mom (0.25 s against 0.03 s above); for three layers its depth-group tables make it fast (a 40 m grid at 1.25 m segments in 0.30 s instead of 66.6 s).
    • fem is a reference, not a production engine. The axisymmetric model takes a fraction of a second for a rod or a ring and about 17 s for eight rings with a central rod (a million nodes). The 3-D model takes 26 s and 1.8 GB for a ring of 12.6 m, 75 … 140 s and 4.3 GB for the AP1 station (28 m of wire), and 2 … 3.5 minutes and up to 3.8 GB for the ring with three rods of B10.
    • Inductive conductors add the earth-return assembly, the same for every engine and the dominant cost of inductive studies (12.2 s for a generated 30-house TN network with a Neumann PEN at four frequencies).

    Every engine stops before allocating anything when a world has more than Engine.max_segments segments (default 20 000, ResourceLimitError) and notes from 5 000 segments on that the solve may take minutes.

    Check your choice¶

    Before a long run, gf.diagnose(world, engine) collects the checks that need no solve: resolution, segment budget, thin wires, close and interpenetrating wires, rod ends and wires at a layer interface, mixed coordinates and overlapping cables (read and act on warnings). After it, gf.compare_engines runs the world through a second engine and gf.convergence_study refines the segment length:

    import groundfield as gf

world = gf.create_world(soil=gf.TwoLayerSoil(rho_1=100.0, rho_2=300.0, h_1=2.0))
gf.create_electrode(world, "grid_mesh", name="grid", corner=(-10.0, -10.0, 0.5), size=(20.0, 20.0), n_x=4, n_y=4)
gf.create_source(world, attached_to="grid", magnitude=1.0)

print(gf.diagnose(world, gf.create_engine()) or "no findings")
report = gf.compare_engines(world, engines={"image": gf.create_engine(), "mom": gf.create_engine(backend="mom")})
print(f"image against mom: {report.deviations['grid']:.2%}")   # 0.92%
study = gf.convergence_study(world, gf.create_engine(), segment_lengths=[1.0, 0.5, 0.25])
print(study[["segment_length_m", "abs_Z"]])                     # 4.8005 Ω at every length

    The grid's wires are straight, so the line kernel of image gives the same impedance at every segment length; mom reads 0.9 % lower, the size of its point-kernel bias. Which pair checks what:

    • image against mom checks the current model (the same kernels).
    • mom against mom_sommerfeld checks the Green's function (the same discretisation).
    • Neither pair checks the discretisation itself; that is what the convergence study and the references of the benchmark suite do.
    • Two names that run the same computation (a deprecated alias and its target) are reported as such by compare_engines: their agreement is not evidence.

    Mathematical and physical context¶

    Below 1 kHz the soil is a conductor whose displacement current is negligible (\(\omega\varepsilon\rho \ll 1\)) and whose skin depth \(\delta = \sqrt{2\rho/(\omega\mu_0)}\) — about 500 m for 100 Ω·m at 1 kHz, more at lower frequencies and higher resistivities — is large against the extent of the electrodes. The galvanic part of the problem is therefore stationary current flow at each frequency. With the conductivity \(\sigma = 1/\rho\) piecewise constant in depth and a current density \(q\) injected by the electrodes, the potential \(\varphi\) satisfies

    \[ -\nabla\cdot\bigl(\sigma(z)\,\nabla\varphi\bigr) = q \quad\text{in the soil } (z > 0),\qquad \frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial z}\Big|_{z=0} = 0,\qquad \varphi \to 0 \text{ for } |\mathbf r|\to\infty . \]

    In words: current is conserved in the soil, no current crosses the soil surface into the air, and the potential vanishes at remote earth. \(\varphi\) and the normal current density \(\sigma\,\partial\varphi/\partial z\) are continuous at every layer interface (\(z\) points into the soil). Frequency enters through the conductors only: their longitudinal impedance (resistance, skin effect, partial inductances and the earth-return correction, see earth return) makes the network equations complex and frequency dependent, while the soil kernel stays real. A galvanic world is therefore solved once and reused for every frequency.

    The Green's function of this problem is the potential of a unit point current at depth \(z_s\), observed at horizontal distance \(s\) and depth \(z\) in the top layer. It is the Sommerfeld integral

    \[ G(s, z; z_s) = \frac{\rho_1}{4\pi}\int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-\lambda|z-z_s|} + e^{-\lambda(z+z_s)} + \Gamma_1(\lambda)\bigl[e^{-\lambda(2h_1-|z-z_s|)} + e^{-\lambda(2h_1-z-z_s)}\bigr]} {1-\Gamma_1(\lambda)\,e^{-2\lambda h_1}}\;J_0(\lambda s)\,\mathrm d\lambda , \]

    a superposition of cylindrical waves \(J_0(\lambda s)\) over the horizontal wavenumber \(\lambda\). Here \(\rho_1\) and \(h_1\) are the resistivity and the thickness of the top layer, and \(\Gamma_1(\lambda)\) is the reflection coefficient of the stack below the first interface, built bottom-up from the interface coefficients \(K_i = (\rho_{i+1}-\rho_i)/(\rho_{i+1}+\rho_i)\). For one layer \(\Gamma_1 = 0\) and \(G\) is the point source plus its air image; for two layers \(\Gamma_1 = K_1\) is constant and the denominator expands into the Tagg / Sunde image series; for three and more layers no closed form exists and the integral is evaluated numerically. The engines differ in how they evaluate \(G\) and in how they discretise the electrodes.

    Two current models¶

    All integral engines discretise every wire into straight segments of length at most Engine.segment_length (and split them at layer interfaces). They then differ in the unknowns:

    • Uniform current per straight wire (image, image_2layer). Each straight wire of an electrode — a full wire line of a grid, an arm of a star, a straight run of a polyline (user decisions Q30 and Q44 of 2026.11), and each electrode per soil layer it reaches — carries one leakage current, spread uniformly per unit length over its segments; the wires of an electrode are ideally bonded. The unknowns are a handful per electrode, the reaction matrix is reduced with length weights to a wire-by-wire multiport matrix, and the solve is cheap. The ansatz is exact for a ring by symmetry and good for impedances of compact electrodes (grids about 1 % above one current per segment); it misplaces current along each wire — towards the tips of a star and the ends of a strip — which matters for surface potentials near the electrode (touch and step voltages) more than for impedances.
    • One current per segment (mom, mom_sommerfeld). Point matching (collocation): one pulse current per segment, the potential matched at every segment midpoint. This resolves how the current distributes along and between electrodes; it uses a point-source near field, which leaves it 1–2 % below the exact thin-wire solution and drifting under refinement (see mom).

    fem is not a third current model of the same problem: it solves the field in the soil by finite elements, the electrodes as the solid bodies they are where the world is axisymmetric and as smoothed line sources in three dimensions otherwise (experimental, see fem).

    Network equations¶

    Electrodes bonded by ideal conductors form a cluster with one potential; finite conductors (lumped, distributed, inductive) are branches between nodes; junctions are nodes without soil contact. Every engine of the integral family assembles the same augmented nodal system — multiport relation between leakage currents and node potentials, Kirchhoff's current law at every node, and Ohm's law along every branch:

    \[ \begin{bmatrix} Z & -C & 0 \\ C^{\top} & 0 & B^{\top} \\ 0 & B & -Z_b(\omega) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf I_e \\ \boldsymbol\varphi_n \\ \mathbf I_b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf 0 \\ \mathbf I_\text{in} \\ \mathbf 0 \end{bmatrix}, \]

    with \(Z\) the soil reaction (multiport) matrix, \(C\) the owner-to-node incidence, \(B\) the branch-to-node incidence and \(Z_b\) the branch impedance matrix; \(\mathbf I_e\) are the leakage currents, \(\boldsymbol\varphi_n\) the node potentials, \(\mathbf I_b\) the branch currents and \(\mathbf I_\text{in}\) the injected source currents. The first row says that the soil turns leakage currents into potentials, the second that current is conserved at every node, the third that each branch obeys Ohm's law. Sources inject \(+I\) at attached_to and, if set, \(-I\) at return_to (two-terminal sources, see sources and measurement). A node without a source floats: it takes the potential the field imposes with zero net current, which keeps transfer impedances reciprocal.

    An impressed voltage source (VoltageSource, since 2026.11) is added by modified nodal analysis: its current \(I_v\) becomes one more unknown, which enters Kirchhoff's current law at attached_to and return_to, and one more row fixes \(\varphi_a - \varphi_r + Z_q I_v = U\) (\(\varphi_r = 0\) for remote earth; \(Z_q = R + j\omega L\) the internal impedance of the source, zero for an ideal source). With the terminal incidence \(S\) and the diagonal matrix \(Z_q(\omega)\) of the internal impedances the system grows by one block row and column,

    \[ \begin{bmatrix} Z & -C & 0 & 0 \\ C^{\top} & 0 & B^{\top} & -S \\ 0 & B & -Z_b(\omega) & 0 \\ 0 & S^{\top} & 0 & Z_q(\omega) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf I_e \\ \boldsymbol\varphi_n \\ \mathbf I_b \\ \mathbf I_v \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf 0 \\ \mathbf I_\text{in} \\ \mathbf 0 \\ \mathbf U \end{bmatrix}, \]

    and the solution of a world without voltage sources is unchanged. A reactive \(Z_q\) makes the system complex at \(f > 0\), so such a world is solved per frequency also without inductive conductors. The mom family borders its segment-level system the same way.

    The registered backends¶

    Backends are registered in groundfield.solver.registry with their solve function, declared capabilities and options model (ADR-0014). Engine.solve resolves the requested name, checks the world against the capabilities before any work is done (soil layer count, concrete shells, explicitly requested kernel; BackendCapabilityError names a backend that can solve the world), forwards image to image_2layer for a two-layer soil, and writes the metadata core (backend, effective backend, kernel, version, frequencies, segments, soil kind, earth model, solve time, series controls, provenance) into every result.     Deprecated names¶

    Three names of earlier releases are deprecated aliases since 2026.11; they keep working, emit a DeprecationWarning naming the replacement, and will be removed no earlier than 2027.5.

    • cim ran the complex-image method: approximate \(\Gamma_1(\lambda)\) by a sum of exponentials (matrix-pencil fit), which turns the Sommerfeld integral into a finite sum of complex images. For one and two layers the closed forms are exact and no fit was ever used on a solve path; the three-layer kernel was structurally incomplete and has raised since 0.11.0. cim now runs the image auto-dispatch, which gives the numbers cim gave on every world it accepted. The fit itself, groundfield.solver.cim.fit_complex_images, remains available for studies of the spectral reflection coefficient; it reports convergence only when the maximum error on an independent grid meets the tolerance.
    • bem was described as a boundary-element collocation but assembled the same reaction matrix as mom (to 4e-16) and now runs mom.
    • image_nlayer routed a layered soil with one or two distinct layers to image / image_2layer and rejected three. image does the routing itself; a real image series for \(n \ge 3\) (Stefanescu's construction) is not implemented (ADR-0002), use mom_sommerfeld.

    Layer stacks are canonicalised before they reach a backend: zero-thickness layers are dropped and adjacent layers of equal resistivity merged, so TwoLayerSoil(ρ, ρ, h) and MultiLayerSoil([ρ, ρ, ρ]) are solved as the homogeneous soil they are (benchmark B9).
