    T07 — The fall-of-potential measurement¶

    The grounding impedance of a substation is measured with the fall-of-potential (three-pole) method: an earth tester drives a test current \(I\) between the earthing system E and an auxiliary current electrode H (German Hilfserder) at a distance \(d\), and measures the voltage between E and a potential probe S (Sonde) placed between them. The reading is

    \[ Z_\mathrm{meas} = \frac{\varphi_E - \varphi_S}{I}. \]

    It equals the true impedance against remote earth only if S sits at the right place. This tutorial simulates the measurement, derives the classical 61.8 % rule and checks it against the field solution, and then adds the measurement leads of a real instrument.

    You will learn

    • how to model a test current that returns through an auxiliary electrode (return_to);
    • how to read a simulated earth tester (gf.measured_impedance) and the two-pole reading (input_impedance);
    • how far the auxiliary electrode must be, and where the probe belongs;
    • how leads ending on the instrument are modelled with junction nodes, and how inductive coupling between the leads enters the reading.

    Runtime: about 20 s.

    The 61.8 % rule in one line¶

    Treat E as a hemisphere of radius \(r\) with \(Z_E = \rho/(2\pi r)\) and H as a small hemisphere at distance \(d\). The test current leaves E and returns into H, so the potentials of E and of a surface point S at distance \(x\) from E are

    \[ \varphi_E = \frac{\rho I}{2\pi}\Bigl(\frac1r - \frac1d\Bigr),\qquad \varphi_S = \frac{\rho I}{2\pi}\Bigl(\frac1x - \frac1{d - x}\Bigr), \]

    and the reading carries the error

    \[ Z_\mathrm{meas} - Z_E = \frac{\rho}{2\pi}\Bigl(-\frac1d - \frac1x + \frac1{d-x}\Bigr). \]

    It vanishes for \(x^2 + xd - d^2 = 0\), i.e. \(x = \frac{\sqrt5 - 1}{2}\,d \approx 0.618\,d\) (Tagg, 1964). With the probe in the middle, \(x = d/2\), the error is \(-\rho/(2\pi d)\): it falls only with \(1/d\).

def measurement_world(d=None, x=None):
    # Station ring E; optional auxiliary electrode H at distance d and probe rod S at x.
    world = gf.create_world(soil=gf.HomogeneousSoil(rho=RHO))
    gf.create_electrode(
        world, "ring", name="station", center=(0.0, 0.0, 0.7), radius=2.0
    )
    if d is not None:

            world, "rod", name="aux", position=(d, 0.0, 0.0), length=1.5
        )
    if x is not None:
        gf.create_electrode(
            world, "rod", name="probe", position=(x, 0.0, 0.0), length=0.5
        )
    # The test current flows into the station and returns through H.
    gf.create_source(
        world,
        attached_to="station",
        return_to="aux" if d is not None else None,
        magnitude=1.0,
    )
    return world

    Simulating the instrument¶

    The fall-of-potential curve¶

    An ideal voltmeter draws no current, so the probe can also be a plain field point: result.potential on the surface between E and H gives the whole curve from one solve. The curve has a flat part around the 61.8 % point if the auxiliary electrode is far enough; the dashed line is the hemisphere formula above with \(Z_E\) from the remote-earth solve.

    How far must the auxiliary electrode be?¶

    We repeat the measurement for \(d\) from 20 m to 1.6 km and record the error of the 50 % and of the 61.8 % probe position. The hemisphere formula predicts \(-\rho/(2\pi d Z_E)\) for 50 % and zero for 61.8 %.

    The 50 % reading follows the \(1/d\) law of the hemisphere formula closely; the 61.8 % reading is far better at every distance — its residual comes from the finite size of the station, which the hemisphere picture ignores. For an extended earthing system (a whole LV network) the "electrical centre" is not known, and both the distance and the probe position need more care; tutorial T06 builds such networks.

    Leads that end on the instrument¶

    A real earth tester stands at the station and is connected to H and S by long leads. Electrically, the end of a lead at the instrument is a node without soil contact. groundfield models it as a junction (gf.create_junction): a network node that takes part in Kirchhoff's law but leaks no current. The generator TnNetworkGenerator builds a whole measurement set-up this way: the feed lead runs from the junction meas_H to the auxiliary electrode, the probe lead from meas_S to the probe, and the source drives the test current between the station and meas_H. gf.measured_impedance(result) then reads \(\varphi_E - \varphi_S\) at the instrument terminal meas_S.

    Resistive leads must not change the three-pole reading (the voltmeter draws no current), while the feed-lead resistance adds to the two-pole reading:

    Inductive coupling between the leads¶

    At 50 Hz the test current in the feed lead induces a voltage in a probe lead that runs beside it; the reading gains an imaginary part. We give both leads their inductance (overhead_lead() uses the Neumann model) and move the probe sideways, so that its lead leaves the feed lead at a growing angle. The earth return is the Sommerfeld model with the faster tabulated assembly. The coupling falls as the leads separate — the reason why probe leads are laid at an angle to the feed lead in practice.

    Summary¶

    • A measurement is a two-terminal source: create_source(..., return_to=...). gf.measured_impedance is the three-pole reading, input_impedance() the two-pole reading.
    • With a compact earthing system the 61.8 % rule removes the error of a finite auxiliary distance almost completely; the 50 % position leaves an error \(\rho/(2\pi d Z_E)\).
    • Leads end on junction nodes at the instrument; resistive leads do not change the three-pole reading, inductive coupling between feed and probe lead does.
